# Prior-Art Comparison: Recipient Retrieval and Covert-Carriage Pipelines

**Checkpoint:** 2026-11-18  
**Role:** Working literature synthesis for W0.3/W6  
**Status:** Theory only · No implementation · Not audited.

## 1. Purpose and inspection boundary

This comparison tests the current candidate contributions against the closest identified work. It is intended to prevent novelty-by-combination and to expose where apparently similar systems solve different problems under different observer assumptions.

The following full texts have been inspected for their stated models, constructions, threat assumptions, evaluation and limitations:

- Liu and Tromer (2022), *Oblivious Message Retrieval* (OMR), including its formal definitions, DoS model, key unlinkability, performance and integration sections.
- Liu, Tromer and Wang (2024), *PerfOMR*, including its adapted OMR definition, adversary model, constructions, benchmarks and Zcash integration discussion.
- Shuhan, Baldimtsi and Ateniese (2026), *Oblivious Signaling*, including the formal receiver-privacy model, server leakage, inbox update construction, cost model and limitations.
- Lee and Yeo (2026), *SophOMR*, including its receiver-linkage model, SIMD-aware compression construction and evaluation.
- Liang et al. (2026), *InstantOMR*, including its OMR threat model, hybrid construction, benchmark methodology and latency/throughput trade-offs. The conference paper defers some formal details and malicious-detector correctness/integrity discussion to its full version; those details are not treated as verified here.
- Bauer et al. (2024), the full version of *Leveraging Generative Models for Covert Messaging: Challenges and Tradeoffs for “Dead-Drop” Deployments*, including its MBFTE construction, rendezvous/key-table arrangement, platform constraints, discovery hints, threat model and empirical evaluation.
- Bauer, Bao and Bindschaedler (2025), *Provably Secure Covert Messaging Using Image-based Diffusion Processes*, including its problem model, pre-shared-key assumption, adversary model, latent-space indistinguishability argument, proof discussion and empirical evaluation.

This is a focused comparison, not a systematic literature review, independent reproduction, proof verification or complete account of all related systems. Full-text inspection means the paper was examined; it does not mean its implementation or claims were independently reproduced.

## 3. Findings that change the novelty assessment

### 3.1 Recipient privacy is already a mature design space

OMR is not simply a generic “hidden inbox” idea. It has formalised message-recipient privacy, malicious-party collusion models, key unlinkability and DoS-resistance variants. PerfOMR, SophOMR and InstantOMR continue to optimise different parts of its cost profile. Oblivious Signaling changes where the expensive privacy work occurs, shifting cost from recipient retrieval to sender-triggered updates.

Consequently, the thesis cannot claim to introduce private recipient retrieval, recipient-message unlinkability, or the general idea of outsourcing discovery to an untrusted service. It must compare the actual alternatives and their different assumptions and cost models.

### 3.2 “Whole-pipeline” analysis is not sufficient by itself

The 2024 dead-drop paper explicitly moves beyond the steganographic core and examines a deployable public-platform pipeline: format transformation, record framing, fragmentation, platform constraints, rendezvous, overt/covert hints, retrieval, user detection and empirical trade-offs.

It therefore overlaps directly with a broad claim that this project is novel merely because it considers the entire delivery lifecycle. Its exclusions are important: the paper assumes the adversary cannot control the platform, observe fine-grained user actions or obtain IP identifiers, and leaves platform cooperation outside scope. Its text-generation channel and record construction also differ from the proposed image-carried protected-object route.

The distinction, if any, must be stated more precisely than “we consider the full pipeline.”

### 3.3 Image-based covert communication exists, but the delivery problem differs

The 2025 diffusion paper provides a formal image-based covert communication construction. It embeds information in generated images' initial latents, assumes a pre-shared key, and analyses a strong distinguisher that knows the model and can invert images. Its formal claim is tied to latent-space distribution preservation and its stated adversary.

However, the authors explicitly leave image delivery to the recipient outside their problem. Their channel is a generated image, not an arbitrary existing image carrying an independently protected capsule. It also prioritises small, high-security messages rather than frequent, low-latency private messaging.

The project must therefore distinguish at least three different ideas: embedding into an existing image, generating an image whose latent state carries information, and carrying an already protected object through an image-based route. They are not interchangeable.

MRAS (SRC-033) and DRAS (SRC-034) then address different JPEG channel regimes rather than a general image-message protocol. MRAS targets matching same-QF recompression through iterative block stabilisation, lattice/frequency selection, STC embedding and parity-based post-processing; DRAS targets downward recompression through quantisation-table-ratio location selection (RoLoS), predictive channel modelling and STC. The full papers remain access-restricted; the publisher-indexed sections and README-labelled author MATLAB repositories have been inspected at source level. Their public demos each process ten sample images as separate per-image operations and simulate the channel with local MATLAB `imwrite` (MRAS defaults to QF80 same-QF; DRAS defaults to QF95-or-higher cover to QF80). Each call takes a prefix of a sample-message file; neither exposed repository defines a cross-image fragment manifest or set reconstruction. DRAS also truncates the requested message if the STC routine reports insufficient embedded bits, so requested and achieved payload must be reported separately. Both demos process the first JPEG component, include Windows `.mexw64` binaries without their build sources, and omit the full benchmark dataset, baseline implementations and platform test harness. These code observations do not disprove the papers' separately reported LinkedIn/WhatsApp/X experiments; those remain author-reported and unreproduced. They show that the available artifacts demonstrate per-image JPEG-channel routines, not multi-image logical-message linkage or a reproduction of the reported live-platform tests.

### 3.4 Multi-image fragmentation and batch steganalysis are established comparators

Wang et al. (2024; SRC-043) directly demonstrate a multi-image fragmented covert-communication framework: a text file is divided into fragments; fragment numbers and content are embedded in images; each fragment is redundantly carried in multiple images; and the receiver reconstructs using sufficient carriers and majority voting over redundant copies. The authors explicitly distinguish steganography from watermarking and use robust DFT/DCT watermarking as the carrier mechanism. This is strong prior art against generic claims to multi-image fragmentation, redundant image carriage or subset-based reconstruction. It is not evidence of endpoint-authenticated message-set semantics, conversation continuity, sender identity or stealth against collection-level analysis.

Ker's batch-steganography work (SRC-044) studies pooling detector evidence across a fixed batch. The separate capacity theorem (SRC-045) establishes an asymptotic square-root bound under explicit assumptions: equal cover capacities, a shift-hypothesis detector response with regularity conditions, independent per-cover errors and batch size known to both parties. The theorem's bounds may be loose; neither work supplies a universal quantitative law for current social-platform images, dependent collections, robust watermarking or sequential acquisition.

**Research consequence:** The project must not claim novelty in multi-image fragmentation or redundant fragment recovery. It must evaluate the whole collection as well as individual carriers, distinguish robust watermarking from steganographic concealment, and ask whether authenticated message-set/context handling, observer-specific linkage analysis or route-neutral package semantics produce a narrower and measurable difference. That remains an open question, not an originality claim.

### 3.5 Privacy claims must be compared at the observer boundary

The reviewed systems protect different relationships:

- OMR-family systems protect message-to-recipient linkage within a public-board/detector model.
- Oblivious Signaling protects receiver linkage at the server while relying on an external anonymous channel for sender anonymity and accepting timing/system leakage.
- The dead-drop pipeline studies whether covert posts and users can be detected on a public platform, but excludes some platform and network observations.
- Diffusion-based covert messaging studies whether an image-generation process reveals embedded communication, while leaving delivery outside scope.

A single phrase such as “transport privacy” or “unlinkability” would conceal these differences. The thesis needs an explicit observer/property matrix: who observes which event, what they can correlate, what they can alter, which parties may collude, and which leakage is deliberately outside the model.

### 3.6 Active sanitization is a distinct adversarial route condition

Zhu et al. (2021; SRC-036) describe a learned attack/optimisation network intended to remove hidden data while preserving image quality. Wei et al. (2022; SRC-046) describe a general secret-data remover for robust steganography and watermarking, including image fusion and latent renewal. Zhu et al. (2023; SRC-047) describe a scaling-aware sanitization framework with Scaling-Net and SC-Net plus a discriminator. These papers establish active payload-removal as a researched countermeasure class; they do not establish that any named platform deploys these models or that the methods defeat every hiding scheme.

Zhu et al. (2025; SRC-048) then describe a counter-adaptation: a secret-seed-conditioned decoder is joined with a sanitizing network, and the sender iteratively optimises the cover so the sanitiser's output yields the intended secret to a receiver with the seed. This is not evidence that an unknown or changing sanitizer can be defeated. It changes the experiment: sanitizer knowledge, version, access, training assumptions and receiver side information become explicit adversary parameters.

**Research consequence:** Separate passive recompression, active sanitization and sender adaptation to a known sanitizer. A successful carrier extraction under one declared model is not a general robustness guarantee; neither extraction nor concealment establishes endpoint authenticity or message privacy.

### 3.7 Multi-frame GIF redundancy is not multi-image message fragmentation

Wang et al. (2026; SRC-049) provide a distinct carrier topology: one animated GIF with 16 frames, with bit repetition within each frame and majority voting across frames. Inspection of the author-linked README and selected code shows per-frame key/nonce material, a local key/ground-truth sidecar workflow, a sender path that writes frame PNGs, and a receiver that asserts the full 16-frame/key set. Missing-frame erasure tolerance is not established. The inspected StreamCipher uses raw ChaCha20 with no authentication tag visible in that path; a 32-bit NumPy PRNG fallback is labelled TEST ONLY. These code-level observations are not a security audit; the paper's formal security claims and result tables have not been inspected.

This is one animated carrier object with internal frame redundancy. It is not a set of separate image posts, an endpoint-authenticated fragment manifest, or a conversation of distinct logical messages. It therefore sharpens the terminology rather than displacing the multi-image prior-art comparison.

**Research consequence:** Compare frame-level loss/distortion and cross-frame recovery separately from separate-object fragmentation, account/time-window collection analysis and conversation linkage. Do not equate frame count with carrier count or message count.

### 3.8 Forensic watermarking and leak attribution: a historical comparison

Sony's role provides a useful historical anchor, with two distinct strands kept separate. In 1999, Sony joined Hitachi, IBM, NEC and Pioneer in the Galaxy group, unifying proposals for invisible digital-video watermarking intended to carry copy-control instructions and survive analogue conversion and repeated compression/decompression (SRC-061). In 2001, Sony joined a wider Video Watermarking Group with Hitachi, NEC, Pioneer, Digimarc, Macrovision and Philips. Separately, reporting on Academy screeners in 2005 describes individualised watermarks identifying the recipient of each copy, so that a leaked screener could be traced to its source. The available evidence does not establish that this screener system was a Sony programme or used the Galaxy/VWM technology; these are related historical examples, not one continuous implementation.

The author reports having worked in Sony R&D during the relevant period. This is firsthand contextual provenance for the historical comparison, not evidence of participation in the Galaxy/VWM work, the Academy screener system, or any particular watermark design.

The technical connection to this research is a shared survivability problem with a different security objective. A forensic watermark is designed to persist through copying and transformation so that an embedded attribution signal remains recoverable. Steganographic carriage seeks to conceal the presence of a payload; robust watermarking may instead make a signal deliberately persistent and detectable by an authorised investigator. Recipient-specific attribution can therefore improve leak tracing while directly increasing linkability. Neither watermark recovery nor recipient attribution supplies endpoint message authentication, sender/recipient identity assurance, confidentiality, multipart integrity or acceptance semantics.

**Research consequence:** Treat payload recoverability, concealment, attribution and unlinkability as separate properties. A robust carrier signal may improve survival while worsening traceability; that tension is a comparative question, not a reason to select watermarking or alter the endpoint-only architecture.

**Thesis-narrative placement:** The historical context is introduced briefly in §2.3 as reflective research provenance, then the technical distinction is foregrounded at the opening of §8.3 alongside SRC-043. This sequencing makes the research evolution legible without implying that Sony's programmes caused the project or that the author participated in them.

### 3.9 Robust JPEG/SNP research lineage and encrypted Telegram carriage

The image-carriage baseline is broader than the MRAS/DRAS papers alone. A related Kumar/Bansal publication line includes an earlier same-quality JPEG method (SRC-065), a high-fidelity JPEG/social-network method reporting 96% recovery after QF95-to-QF70 recompression in its abstract (SRC-064), CEDAR's downward-recompression method using cover and anticipated-channel quantisation tables without ECC (SRC-063), and the later MRAS/DRAS papers (SRC-033/034). The associated SNP StegoLab repository (SRC-062) brings together Python paths for MRAS/DRAS, a fixed-grid QIM method with Reed–Solomon error correction, and local/Telegram/Discord test adapters. These are related publications and a companion code artifact from a shared author/account lineage, not independent replications. Exact method overlap between the papers remains a full-text question.

A separate 2026 IEEE conference paper by Khegay et al. (SRC-066) is a particularly direct comparator. Its publisher abstract describes AES-256-GCM, RSA-based key encapsulation, RS(255,223) forward error correction and DCT image embedding, with Telegram photo-mode testing. It therefore defeats any broad novelty claim that combining cryptographic payload protection, error correction, robust image embedding and Telegram carriage is itself new. The abstract reports a 100% success rate for its stated 50–150-byte evaluation and separately describes high recovery in Telegram photo mode; it does not explicitly attribute the 100% figure to Telegram. It also reports that payloads of 200 bytes or more exceeded capacity and that resizing remains problematic.

The accessible abstracts do not establish an authenticated multi-carrier fragment-set protocol, route-neutral transport semantics, cross-route equivalence, conversation continuity or a common endpoint-acceptance model. Those properties must remain open questions, not assumed omissions or novelty claims. Full text and methods remain unavailable for SRC-063–SRC-066, and no result has been reproduced here.

**Research consequence:** The image route cannot be defended as novel merely because it combines encryption, error correction, robust DCT embedding or Telegram carriage. Any remaining Candidate B/C distinction must be demonstrated at the level of cross-route semantics, authenticated relationships, endpoint acceptance and observer/evidence comparison, with a decision-relevant result beyond the carrier-level work.

### 3.10 SNP StegoLab: companion implementation and test-harness limits

SRC-062 is useful because it exposes an integrated code path from per-image embedding through attempted channel carriage and extraction. It does not supply an independent reproduction of MRAS or DRAS: the repository is hosted by the same GitHub account as the already inspected author-published MATLAB repositories, and its own README and code are not independent experimental evidence.

Static source inspection identifies several reporting and portability limitations. The batch runner skips failed embedding, channel and extraction cases and calculates its summary from the completed results only. Its BER helper truncates unequal bit vectors to their common prefix. PSNR and SSIM are measured before channel transmission, so they do not describe the quality of the platform-returned image. The Telegram adapter uses a fixed two-second wait; the Discord adapter retrieves a proxy URL without establishing that a transformation occurred. The MRAS module executes matrix loading at import from a hard-coded external Windows path to a `common.cpp` file absent from the repository tree; the batch tester and worker module import that module at load time. No test suite, recorded report or dependency lockfile is present in the inspected tree.

These are static source-inspection findings, not reproduced execution failures. They limit the evidential weight of the repository's README performance claims and make it unsuitable as independent confirmation of the underlying papers. Its visible workflow is per-image carrier testing; the inspected code does not define an authenticated cross-image fragment manifest, set reconstruction or conversation linkage. It therefore establishes that basic image-carrier test automation is represented in prior art, not that the wider endpoint and cross-route questions have been resolved.

## 4.1 Server-dependent retrieval: comparative boundary

The following systems are examined as prior art only. Their service roles, databases, publication boards, indexes, addresses, capabilities and retrieval procedures are not components of The Invisible Postman. The comparison asks what they achieve and what external dependencies and observer assumptions those achievements require.

APIR (SRC-054) provides multi-server authenticated point and predicate/aggregate queries under an at-least-one-honest-server model; its single-server construction is point-query only and relies on an out-of-band digest. vPIR and multi-query vPIR verify properties or multiple known point answers, not arbitrary hidden-predicate result-set enumeration. AAD supports complete keyed lookup and append-only/fork-consistent evolution but exposes ordinary lookup paths and has no authenticated submitter identity. The 2024 encrypted range-query schemes and 2026 range APIR are direct prior art for privacy-preserving/verifiable range retrieval, subject to their different trust and leakage models. SRC-056 remains indexed-text-only and SRC-059 remains abstract/introduction-only.

The APIR artifact inspection adds a concrete implementation boundary. At pinned commit `7a7f2041bc1e0907ef089f8e8e55252ef7f18e0f`, `serverFSS.answer` iterates through `db.NumColumns`, evaluates the FSS predicate against each stored record and accumulates an authenticated scalar aggregate. The client reconstructs and authenticates that aggregate; it receives no matching record identifiers. The separate point-PIR client accepts an index already supplied by the caller and retrieves that indexed record. It does not discover unknown matching indices. The gRPC server loads a local PGP-key population at startup and exposes database-info and query RPCs. The README warns that the software is not production-ready. The pointVPIR Merkle path uses precomputed record proofs and a root obtained through the server's database-info path; the inspected artifact does not establish sender-authenticated admission or a self-contained multipart message protocol.

The result is bounded: APIR's predicate COUNT and point-query primitive do not combine into a self-contained message mechanism. A count reveals cardinality, not locations; point PIR retrieves an index the client already knows. Enumerating unknown matches requires the server-side population and some additional index-discovery mechanism or a scan. This is a property of the examined retrieval design, not a missing subsystem to add to The Invisible Postman.

## 4.2 Retired server-dependent retrieval hypothesis: comparative boundary

The former Candidate D is retired as an architectural candidate and contribution hypothesis. Its formal model is not a subsystem to build, improve or integrate. It is retained only to make explicit what server-dependent private/verifiable retrieval systems assume, what their proofs establish, and why those assumptions are outside The Invisible Postman's endpoint-only message architecture.

The governing requirement is different: a protected message is a standalone object. The object and its authenticated parts carry the information needed for endpoint validation, decryption and reconstruction. The recipient uses endpoint-held keys and local trust state. No publication service, server-side database, index, fixed address, rendezvous point, coordinator or external resolver is required to establish message identity, associate fragments, reconstruct the protected object or accept it. External systems may carry or host bytes, but they do not become authorities over message relationships.

The examined APIR predicate COUNT and point-query primitives do not form a self-contained message mechanism: the count reports cardinality, not the matching locations, and the point query retrieves an index already known to the client. The broader OMR/PIR, AAD and range-query literature remains relevant to comparing service-dependent discovery systems, but it does not create a requirement to reproduce or improve their infrastructure.

**Disposition:** former Candidate D retired. No composition, database subsystem, server-side index, publication service or retrieval protocol is pursued for The Invisible Postman. Preserve the cited prior-art boundaries and use them only when comparing the external delivery assumptions of other systems.

## 5. Candidate C decision-relevance and route/fault evidence

Candidate C is distinct from the retired Candidate D server-dependent retrieval hypothesis. Candidate C remains a conditional cross-route evaluation method; the former Candidate D is retained only as comparative prior-art analysis. The decision test below must not be read as evidence that any route has won.

### 5.1 Decision-relevance test and candidate boundary

The crosswalk now supports a narrower conclusion than a novel receiver-acceptance mechanism. Authenticated manifests, membership checks, completeness rules, whole-object integrity, freshness and replay handling are ordinary endpoint protocol obligations. Stateful private retrieval, retrieval-window completeness and retryable cursors are also represented in prior art (SRC-050–SRC-052). Re-labelling these obligations as a route-independent acceptance contract would not establish originality. The contract remains useful as a comparison discipline, but its mechanism-level novelty claim is retired unless a specific gap survives stronger prior-art review.

The supplied camera-ready StOMR paper sharpens, rather than changes, this boundary. It defines S-OMR as evolving sender/receiver state across repeated signalling and decoding, with the detection key refreshed during decoding; its OMR-KEM wraps an underlying OMR signal with public-key encryption of a session key so the parties can establish shared key material without a pre-existing out-of-band channel. These are concrete prior-art mechanisms for private recipient retrieval and state bootstrap. They do not authenticate a human contact identity, establish global publication/candidate-view completeness, or validate a multi-carrier logical-message manifest. The stated adversary is passive and protocol-following, with broad party collusion except the target sender/receiver pair; active publication, detector deviation and service omission are outside that model. The state syntax also should not be mistaken for a specified production recovery protocol for counter rollback, forks or concurrent sender state.

A concrete-parameter caution is necessary. StOMR's evaluation fixes PRF signal outputs at 32 bits while testing bulletin boards up to 2^16 entries. Appendix C.1's generic cross-user collision bound is `binom(|BB|, 2) / 2^m`; at those parameters the bound is approximately 0.5. This loose all-pairs bound is not the probability of a false match for one particular receiver, and the paper does not supply a tighter concrete advantage estimate or collision stress test for that largest board. Its ≥128-bit BFV parameter statement must therefore not be read as a 128-bit bound on the separate 32-bit signal-collision term. This is a parameter-specific evidence caveat, not a refutation of the asymptotic theorem.

UnifOMR adds a different but equally important boundary, now directly checked against the supplied 56-page primary paper (SRC-051). Its Theorem 5.1 shows that strongly detection-key-unlinkable OMR implies PIR with essentially no online overhead, subject to a stated 1/N² reduction loss; the paper also relates OMR to key-private PKE with wrong-key decryption. UnifOMR uses RLWE clue encryption, BFV additive homomorphism for partial clue decryption, recipient-side range checks and a black-box batch-PIR stage to retrieve candidate payloads. The detector still scans all clues on the board for each recipient; the two-round design reduces concrete costs but has an asymptotically linear digest and recipient work. The paper's own model separates honest-but-curious completeness/soundness from privacy against malicious/colluding parties. It expressly leaves malicious single-server detector integrity open, so privacy does not establish correct or complete candidate output, and the public-board input does not establish publication or index completeness.

A material issue is visible in the primary text. Algorithm 1 pads the candidate set to k̄ when smaller, but randomly samples k̄ entries when the range-check candidate set exceeds k̄. Definition 3.1 bounds k̄ by the number of true pertinent messages, while false-positive candidates are additional entries; Theorem 6.1's completeness proof does not explain how truncation preserves every true match. At N = 2^19, the stated per-item false-positive bounds permit expected false-positive counts up to about 16 (Param1, ϵp = 2^-15) or 0.125 (Param2, ϵp = 2^-22), by summing the individual bounds. With T = k̄ = 50, lossless retrieval appears to require candidate capacity B ≥ T + F, or an explicit false-positive slack/overflow/retry rule. This is a paper-level proof/algorithm accounting ambiguity, not a demonstrated implementation failure; no joint candidate-distribution experiment or author clarification has been obtained. SRC-053 independently raised the same issue, and its arithmetic is now cross-checked against the primary text.

The authors report 25s/41s detector time and 4,163KB/5,955KB digest size for the representative N = 2^19, k = k̄ = 50, 612-byte case, with 28ms/54ms recipient time and two rounds; these are not reproduced. The paper states that its correctness guarantee assumes a semi-honest detector, while privacy is proven against malicious servers, and identifies malicious-detector integrity and stronger CCA-like security as open questions. Its cited repository currently points to a 2022 proof-of-concept README for the 2021 OMR paper, with no identifiable UnifOMR or VecBatchPIR source in the inspected tree. This bounded artifact check does not prove no separate implementation exists elsewhere.

Together, StOMR and UnifOMR make it untenable to treat private recipient retrieval, evolving retrieval state, compact detection, PIR-based payload selection or retryable retrieval windows as novel mechanisms. They do not collapse the properties into one guarantee: recipient-pertinence privacy, candidate-output correctness/completeness, payload retrieval, publication completeness and endpoint-authenticated logical-message acceptance remain distinct.

The conventional service profiles also narrow generic delivery-evidence claims. Signal's pinned public server snapshot already has per-device message queues, client acknowledgements, queue removal and conditional server delivery receipts; the product distinguishes those from optional Read receipts, and its server source gives queue-empty a bounded per-device meaning. This is not a global all-recipient transaction or proof of human comprehension. Dropbox already exposes owner/editor file activity and configurable viewer information with documented role, plan, Guest, link and direct-download limitations. These native service features do not amount to endpoint-authenticated multi-carrier acceptance, but they show that delivery and access evidence are not absent from conventional services. The remaining distinction must be stated more narrowly than “existing services have no evidence”: the question is whether a cross-route evaluator can reconcile these different native events with authenticated endpoint state and identify a decision-relevant ambiguity beyond the documented semantics (SRC-038–SRC-039).

The documentary route audit of SRC-038–SRC-040 now sharpens the comparison without changing that contribution boundary. Signal's PQXDH/Double Ratchet specifications, Support documentation and pinned public server-source snapshot expose distinct protocol, per-device queue, delivery-acknowledgement, optional Read-receipt and linked-device lifecycle layers; they do not provide a deployed-service/account audit. Dropbox's current documentation distinguishes direct invitation, shared-folder roles and view-only links, and shows that owner-facing viewer information is conditional on role, plan, settings, Guest/sign-in state and preview/download path; it does not establish the provider's complete event record. Pigeonhole's reader-visibility question remains a designed but unexecuted probe. These source findings improve the route/fault model but produce no measured route ranking, no demonstrated image-carriage advantage and no decision-changing result.

The only live Candidate C is therefore a **reproducible cross-route evaluation method**: hold the protected logical object, endpoint validation policy and declared use case constant; preserve each route's native unit and semantics; inject equivalent delivery faults; and record separately (a) route-native operation/result, (b) endpoint-authenticated state transition, and (c) evidence available to each named observer. The method must not force an artificial common transaction onto a single-file host, per-message ratchet, OMR retrieval service, multi-image carrier set or capability-addressed asynchronous store.

A minimum decision test should include:

1. **Common endpoint fixture:** one authenticated protected object, with a declared manifest only where fragmentation is actually used. Record identity/session scope, freshness/replay policy, completeness rule and resource limits before comparing routes.
2. **Native-route baselines:** three primary treatments: one single-file object (Dropbox profile), capability-addressed asynchronous copy (Pigeonhole profile), and a multi-image fragmented/redundant object using SRC-043's native distinction between byte recovery and complete-set acceptance. Test `katzenqt` separately as an application assembler. Signal remains a protocol/service reference profile, not a primary set-atomicity treatment unless a comparable product-level set operation is established. OMR/StOMR/UnifOMR are discovery/retrieval comparators, not interchangeable storage or message-set transactions.
3. **Staged discriminating cases:** first establish complete-delivery controls. Then test the route-specific omission conditions most likely to expose a difference: loss of one redundant copy versus loss of every copy of one SRC-043 fragment; and Pigeonhole E1 success followed by E2 rejection while a recipient concurrently reads. Dropbox and Signal product/service observations require controlled accounts/devices and must remain documentary if those are unavailable. Treat OMR/StOMR/UnifOMR as retrieval-model diagnostics, not storage-route substitutes. Add duplicate, conflict, reorder and replay cases only where the native route semantics and declared use case make them decision-relevant.
4. **Three evidence ledgers:** endpoint ground truth and authenticated transitions; route/service reports with their asserting authority and scope; and observer-visible/inferred events with vantage, collection window, side information and collusion assumptions. Preserve missing evidence as unknown, not as a negative event.
5. **Decision output:** state whether the route satisfies the declared use case, what failure remains detectable or ambiguous, what evidence supports that judgement, and the measured costs (latency, bytes, storage, processing, user effort and route-specific exposure) where those costs can actually be measured.

The method is decision-relevant only if a controlled result or rigorous counterexample changes a stated route choice, invalidates a material assurance claim, or exposes a failure/evidence ambiguity that the closest prior art does not already make explicit. A more detailed table, a longer state machine or the discovery of an undocumented feature is not enough. If the result is simply that every route needs standard endpoint validation and offers different native service evidence, Candidate C should be retained as a useful thesis synthesis, not claimed as an original contribution. If the method cannot be executed against pinned configurations, the thesis must report a documentary comparison and leave the contribution claim unresolved.

This boundary also prevents a category error in the OMR comparison: private candidate retrieval can reduce what a retrieval service learns about recipient pertinence, but it does not establish that the recipient's candidate view is globally complete, that every expected carrier was published, or that a multi-carrier logical object has been accepted. Conversely, a complete endpoint manifest does not prove private discovery or prevent an intermediary from withholding candidates. These are separate properties and require separate evidence.
