# Prior-Art Comparison: Recipient Retrieval and Covert-Carriage Pipelines

**Checkpoint:** 2026-11-18  
**Role:** Working literature synthesis for W0.3/W6  
**Status:** Theory only · No implementation · Not audited.

## 1. Purpose and inspection boundary

This comparison tests the current candidate contributions against the closest identified work. It is intended to prevent novelty-by-combination and to expose where apparently similar systems solve different problems under different observer assumptions.

The following full texts have been inspected for their stated models, constructions, threat assumptions, evaluation and limitations:

- Liu and Tromer (2022), *Oblivious Message Retrieval* (OMR), including its formal definitions, DoS model, key unlinkability, performance and integration sections.
- Liu, Tromer and Wang (2024), *PerfOMR*, including its adapted OMR definition, adversary model, constructions, benchmarks and Zcash integration discussion.
- Shuhan, Baldimtsi and Ateniese (2026), *Oblivious Signaling*, including the formal receiver-privacy model, server leakage, inbox update construction, cost model and limitations.
- Lee and Yeo (2026), *SophOMR*, including its receiver-linkage model, SIMD-aware compression construction and evaluation.
- Liang et al. (2026), *InstantOMR*, including its OMR threat model, hybrid construction, benchmark methodology and latency/throughput trade-offs. The conference paper defers some formal details and malicious-detector correctness/integrity discussion to its full version; those details are not treated as verified here.
- Bauer et al. (2024), the full version of *Leveraging Generative Models for Covert Messaging: Challenges and Tradeoffs for “Dead-Drop” Deployments*, including its MBFTE construction, rendezvous/key-table arrangement, platform constraints, discovery hints, threat model and empirical evaluation.
- Bauer, Bao and Bindschaedler (2025), *Provably Secure Covert Messaging Using Image-based Diffusion Processes*, including its problem model, pre-shared-key assumption, adversary model, latent-space indistinguishability argument, proof discussion and empirical evaluation.

This is a focused comparison, not a systematic literature review, independent reproduction, proof verification or complete account of all related systems. Full-text inspection means the paper was examined; it does not mean its implementation or claims were independently reproduced.

## 3. Findings that change the novelty assessment

### 3.1 Recipient privacy is already a mature design space

OMR is not simply a generic “hidden inbox” idea. It has formalised message-recipient privacy, malicious-party collusion models, key unlinkability and DoS-resistance variants. PerfOMR, SophOMR and InstantOMR continue to optimise different parts of its cost profile. Oblivious Signaling changes where the expensive privacy work occurs, shifting cost from recipient retrieval to sender-triggered updates.

Consequently, the thesis cannot claim to introduce private recipient retrieval, recipient-message unlinkability, or the general idea of outsourcing discovery to an untrusted service. It must compare the actual alternatives and their different assumptions and cost models.

### 3.2 “Whole-pipeline” analysis is not sufficient by itself

The 2024 dead-drop paper explicitly moves beyond the steganographic core and examines a deployable public-platform pipeline: format transformation, record framing, fragmentation, platform constraints, rendezvous, overt/covert hints, retrieval, user detection and empirical trade-offs.

It therefore overlaps directly with a broad claim that this project is novel merely because it considers the entire delivery lifecycle. Its exclusions are important: the paper assumes the adversary cannot control the platform, observe fine-grained user actions or obtain IP identifiers, and leaves platform cooperation outside scope. Its text-generation channel and record construction also differ from the proposed image-carried protected-object route.

The distinction, if any, must be stated more precisely than “we consider the full pipeline.”

### 3.3 Image-based covert communication exists, but the delivery problem differs

The 2025 diffusion paper provides a formal image-based covert communication construction. It embeds information in generated images' initial latents, assumes a pre-shared key, and analyses a strong distinguisher that knows the model and can invert images. Its formal claim is tied to latent-space distribution preservation and its stated adversary.

However, the authors explicitly leave image delivery to the recipient outside their problem. Their channel is a generated image, not an arbitrary existing image carrying an independently protected capsule. It also prioritises small, high-security messages rather than frequent, low-latency private messaging.

The project must therefore distinguish at least three different ideas: embedding into an existing image, generating an image whose latent state carries information, and carrying an already protected object through an image-based route. They are not interchangeable.

MRAS (SRC-033) and DRAS (SRC-034) then address different JPEG channel regimes rather than a general image-message protocol. MRAS targets matching same-QF recompression through iterative block stabilisation, lattice/frequency selection, STC embedding and parity-based post-processing; DRAS targets downward recompression through quantisation-table-ratio location selection (RoLoS), predictive channel modelling and STC. The full papers remain access-restricted; the publisher-indexed sections and README-labelled author MATLAB repositories have been inspected at source level. Their public demos each process ten sample images as separate per-image operations and simulate the channel with local MATLAB `imwrite` (MRAS defaults to QF80 same-QF; DRAS defaults to QF95-or-higher cover to QF80). Each call takes a prefix of a sample-message file; neither exposed repository defines a cross-image fragment manifest or set reconstruction. DRAS also truncates the requested message if the STC routine reports insufficient embedded bits, so requested and achieved payload must be reported separately. Both demos process the first JPEG component, include Windows `.mexw64` binaries without their build sources, and omit the full benchmark dataset, baseline implementations and platform test harness. These code observations do not disprove the papers' separately reported LinkedIn/WhatsApp/X experiments; those remain author-reported and unreproduced. They show that the available artifacts demonstrate per-image JPEG-channel routines, not multi-image logical-message linkage or a reproduction of the reported live-platform tests.

### 3.5 Multi-image fragmentation and batch steganalysis are established comparators

Wang et al. (2024; SRC-043) directly demonstrate a multi-image fragmented covert-communication framework: a text file is divided into fragments; fragment numbers and content are embedded in images; each fragment is redundantly carried in multiple images; and the receiver reconstructs using sufficient carriers and majority voting over redundant copies. The authors explicitly distinguish steganography from watermarking and use robust DFT/DCT watermarking as the carrier mechanism. This is strong prior art against generic claims to multi-image fragmentation, redundant image carriage or subset-based reconstruction. It is not evidence of endpoint-authenticated message-set semantics, conversation continuity, sender identity or stealth against collection-level analysis.

Ker's batch-steganography work (SRC-044) studies pooling detector evidence across a fixed batch. The separate capacity theorem (SRC-045) establishes an asymptotic square-root bound under explicit assumptions: equal cover capacities, a shift-hypothesis detector response with regularity conditions, independent per-cover errors and batch size known to both parties. The theorem's bounds may be loose; neither work supplies a universal quantitative law for current social-platform images, dependent collections, robust watermarking or sequential acquisition.

**Research consequence:** The project must not claim novelty in multi-image fragmentation or redundant fragment recovery. It must evaluate the whole collection as well as individual carriers, distinguish robust watermarking from steganographic concealment, and ask whether authenticated message-set/context handling, observer-specific linkage analysis or route-neutral package semantics produce a narrower and measurable difference. That remains an open question, not an originality claim.

### 3.4 Privacy claims must be compared at the observer boundary

The reviewed systems protect different relationships:

- OMR-family systems protect message-to-recipient linkage within a public-board/detector model.
- Oblivious Signaling protects receiver linkage at the server while relying on an external anonymous channel for sender anonymity and accepting timing/system leakage.
- The dead-drop pipeline studies whether covert posts and users can be detected on a public platform, but excludes some platform and network observations.
- Diffusion-based covert messaging studies whether an image-generation process reveals embedded communication, while leaving delivery outside scope.

A single phrase such as “transport privacy” or “unlinkability” would conceal these differences. The thesis needs an explicit observer/property matrix: who observes which event, what they can correlate, what they can alter, which parties may collude, and which leakage is deliberately outside the model.

### 3.6 Active sanitization is a distinct adversarial route condition

Zhu et al. (2021; SRC-036) describe a learned attack/optimisation network intended to remove hidden data while preserving image quality. Wei et al. (2022; SRC-046) describe a general secret-data remover for robust steganography and watermarking, including image fusion and latent renewal. Zhu et al. (2023; SRC-047) describe a scaling-aware sanitization framework with Scaling-Net and SC-Net plus a discriminator. These papers establish active payload-removal as a researched countermeasure class; they do not establish that any named platform deploys these models or that the methods defeat every hiding scheme.

Zhu et al. (2025; SRC-048) then describe a counter-adaptation: a secret-seed-conditioned decoder is joined with a sanitizing network, and the sender iteratively optimises the cover so the sanitiser's output yields the intended secret to a receiver with the seed. This is not evidence that an unknown or changing sanitizer can be defeated. It changes the experiment: sanitizer knowledge, version, access, training assumptions and receiver side information become explicit adversary parameters.

**Research consequence:** Separate passive recompression, active sanitization and sender adaptation to a known sanitizer. A successful carrier extraction under one declared model is not a general robustness guarantee; neither extraction nor concealment establishes endpoint authenticity or message privacy.

### 3.7 Multi-frame GIF redundancy is not multi-image message fragmentation

Wang et al. (2026; SRC-049) provide a distinct carrier topology: one animated GIF with 16 frames, with bit repetition within each frame and majority voting across frames. Inspection of the author-linked README and selected code shows per-frame key/nonce material, a local key/ground-truth sidecar workflow, a sender path that writes frame PNGs, and a receiver that asserts the full 16-frame/key set. Missing-frame erasure tolerance is not established. The inspected StreamCipher uses raw ChaCha20 with no authentication tag visible in that path; a 32-bit NumPy PRNG fallback is labelled TEST ONLY. These code-level observations are not a security audit; the paper's formal security claims and result tables have not been inspected.

This is one animated carrier object with internal frame redundancy. It is not a set of separate image posts, an endpoint-authenticated fragment manifest, or a conversation of distinct logical messages. It therefore sharpens the terminology rather than displacing the multi-image prior-art comparison.

**Research consequence:** Compare frame-level loss/distortion and cross-frame recovery separately from separate-object fragmentation, account/time-window collection analysis and conversation linkage. Do not equate frame count with carrier count or message count.

## 4.1 Candidate D: the composition boundary

Candidate D is deliberately narrower than "authenticated retrieval" or "complete message delivery." It asks whether a recipient can verify a complete hidden-predicate result relative to a declared publication snapshot and then validate the retrieved carriers against a sender-committed logical-object manifest, while preserving OMR-style recipient privacy. The prior art now directly covers several apparent subproblems. APIR (SRC-054) provides multi-server authenticated point and predicate/aggregate queries under an at-least-one-honest-server model; its single-server construction is point-query only and requires an out-of-band database digest; vPIR (SRC-057) proves local/global database properties while hiding a queried index; multi-query vPIR (SRC-058) binds multiple point answers to one database. Two 2024 encrypted-range-query papers also claim privacy-preserving range search with verifiable result checking: Nie et al.'s VPRQ uses 0/1 encoding, an encrypted garbled Bloom filter and point-value polynomials (SRC-059, abstract/introduction only); Basudan and Alamer use an encrypted oblivious Bloom filter and point-value polynomials (SRC-060, full paper reviewed), but assume trusted data owners/seekers and a primarily semi-honest cloud, and the inspected formal theorem proves IND-SCPA trapdoor privacy rather than a separate malicious-cloud soundness theorem. Youssef et al.'s 2026 APIR range-query paper (SRC-056) explicitly claims soundness and completeness for variable-size private range results over a static owner-signed database. Its indexed text acknowledges leakage from query-dependent access count, timing and communication volume, and its full PDF remains inaccessible in this review. AAD (SRC-055) proves complete keyed lookup and append-only/fork-consistent evolution; it notes VRF compatibility for path derivation, but does not provide PIR-style access-pattern privacy or authenticated submitter identity. OMR supplies recipient-pertinence privacy; SRC-052 supplies service-scoped completeness flags and retryable cursors. Candidate D is not established by placing these names next to each other.

### Composition test against the current model

The reviewed primitives do not simply add up to the Candidate D target:

1. **APIR + AAD:** AAD can return all values for a known key, but it does not provide PIR-style access-pattern hiding or authenticated submitter identity. APIR's authenticated predicate queries return aggregates, not a variable-size list of all matching records; its point-query interface does not supply the missing enumeration index. A naïve composition therefore leaves both hidden-predicate completeness and admission identity unresolved.
2. **vPIR + multi-query vPIR:** property proofs and same-database consistency can authenticate selected rows or global facts such as a count. They do not identify and return every row satisfying a hidden predicate. Enumerating candidate indices would require another complete index-discovery mechanism or a scan whose cost/leakage must be modelled.
3. **Range APIR + AAD:** for a static ordered predicate, range APIR claims sound/complete result retrieval relative to an owner-signed ADS. It exposes query-dependent access count/timing/volume in the indexed description, and it has no append-only admission receipts or sender manifest. AAD adds append-only/keyed completeness but not sender identity, query privacy or dynamic multi-writer admission.
4. **VPRQ / leakage-suppressed range query:** these show that encrypted range search and result verification are established research directions. Their reviewed models are range-specific; the Basudan/Alamer paper trusts the data owner and seeker and is primarily semi-honest for the cloud. Neither reviewed paper defines OMR's recipient-pertinence semantics, a dynamic authenticated publication epoch or multi-carrier sender-manifest closure.
5. **OMR + sender manifest:** OMR supplies hidden recipient pertinence, while a manifest can define expected carriers. Neither alone proves a complete view against a malicious detector or proves that every manifest member was admitted. UnifOMR's fixed-capacity candidate truncation remains a separate completeness-accounting ambiguity; StOMR's passive model does not fill the active-integrity gap.
6. **SRC-052 service completeness:** the integration's complete flag, slot map and retryable cursor describe a service range window. They do not constitute a cryptographic proof of all matching publication records or a sender-authenticated logical-object manifest.

This is a comparison of the reviewed constructions, not an impossibility proof or an exhaustive claim that no composition exists. The current evidence supports a sharply scoped research question, not a novelty claim.

The composition must answer four separate questions: who commits the expected logical object; how publication admission is evidenced; how the detector proves no matching admitted record was omitted without learning the predicate; and how the endpoint verifies set membership, version, freshness and acceptance. The 2024 encrypted-range-query schemes and 2026 APIR range-query construction are direct counterexamples to any broad claim that private, verifiable range retrieval is absent. The 2024 schemes use encrypted indexes and polynomial/Bloom-filter verification; the Basudan/Alamer paper's threat model and formal privacy proof are narrower than a fully malicious-server, access-pattern-hiding guarantee. The 2026 APIR range paper authenticates a range result relative to a static owner-signed ADS, but repeated PIR-Get operations expose query-dependent access count/timing/communication volume. None of these reviewed range schemes defines sender-authenticated multi-carrier admission or endpoint manifest closure. APIR/vPIR's point, aggregate and multi-query properties likewise must not be inflated into arbitrary result-set enumeration. AAD proves all values registered for a key, but the AAS model has no authenticated user identities; append-only/fork consistency does not prove that every externally intended value was submitted, and fork detection relies on external view comparison/gossip whose network assumptions the paper does not model. A public manifest or visible set token may solve collection while defeating unlinkability. An authenticated record proves record authenticity, not necessarily completeness of a hidden-predicate result.

Candidate D should advance only if a formal model and comparison show that the OMR-specific dynamic-predicate/admission/manifest composition supplies a property not already provided by VPRQ, the 2024 leakage-suppressed range scheme, range APIR, vPIR, multi-query vPIR, AAD, OMR or the downstream integration, and if privacy, proof-size, computation, interaction and failure costs remain useful. The alternative outcomes are still valuable: an impossibility/trade-off result, a demonstration that query-volume leakage or a transparent manifest defeats the privacy objective, or a finding that the primitives compose only with prohibitive cost. The UnifOMR candidate-capacity ambiguity and StOMR state-recovery questions are supporting research questions, not substitutes for this main test.

### 4.2 Candidate D: formal property model and negative boundary

Candidate D is a conjunction of distinct properties, not a generic claim of “authenticated private retrieval”. Let \(S\) be the sender, \(R\) the recipient, \(P\) the publication/admission/log service, \(D_1\ldots D_k\) the OMR/PIR retrieval services, and \(C_j\) the carrier platforms. Let \(M_o\) be a sender-authenticated manifest for logical object \(o\); \(DB_e\) the publication database at authenticated epoch \(e\), committed by root \(r_e\) and checkpoint \(\sigma_e\); \(Q_R\) the recipient's hidden pertinence predicate; and \(X_e=\{x\in DB_e:Q_R(x)=1\}\) the exact matching set at that epoch. Let \(K_e\) be the raw candidate set returned by retrieval, \(Y_e\) the final locally filtered and verified result, and \(\pi\) the proof material.

| Obligation | Required statement | What it does **not** establish by itself |
|---|---|---|
| Candidate provenance | Every \(k\in K_e\) is committed in \(DB_e\) | That every matching record was returned |
| Retrieval completeness | \(X_e\subseteq K_e\), for the declared predicate and snapshot | That the snapshot contains every carrier the sender intended |
| Snapshot consistency | All records, proofs and queries bind to the same \(r_e\)/epoch | Global agreement if clients are shown forks and never compare roots |
| Local filtering and final soundness | \(Y_e=\operatorname{Filter}_{Q_R}(K_e)=X_e\) | That a raw candidate set containing false positives is itself an accepted result |
| Append/fork consistency | Later roots extend the declared earlier view; divergent histories cannot silently merge | Availability or inclusion of an event never submitted |
| Query privacy | A permitted coalition's view is simulatable from an explicit leakage function \(L\) | Network anonymity, account anonymity or protection against a coalition outside the stated threshold |
| Admission evidence | Each manifest-declared publication has a verifiable acceptance/inclusion receipt by a stated deadline, or its non-admission is attributable | That the sender's manifest captures unexpressed intent |
| Manifest authenticity/closure | The endpoint verifies sender signature, recipient/session scope, unique fragment membership, coding/recovery rule and whole-object digest | That a carrier was actually admitted or remained available |
| Endpoint acceptance | The recipient accepts only after freshness, membership, completeness and object validation | Human reading, delivery to another device, or a service-side event |

**Sender intent, admission and snapshot completeness are different claims.** An authenticated snapshot can prove what is in the committed database; an AAD lookup can prove all values registered for a key in that dictionary; neither can prove that an intended but never-submitted carrier existed. A sender signature proves the manifest the sender actually committed, not the sender's unexpressed intention. A service-signed receipt can attest that the service acknowledged a submission, but inclusion in the declared snapshot still needs a verifiable inclusion proof or deadline rule; a malicious service can always deny availability.

This yields a limited indistinguishability boundary. Suppose a verifier receives only an authenticated snapshot \(DB_e\) and proofs derived from it, with no independently delivered sender-authenticated manifest or admission receipt. In world \(W_0\), the sender intended exactly the records in \(DB_e\). In world \(W_1\), the sender intended those same records plus a carrier \(z\), but \(z\) was never submitted and the committed snapshot is unchanged. The verifier's authenticated view is identical in both worlds. It therefore cannot prove “all sender-intended carriers were published”; it can prove only completeness relative to the committed snapshot or to a manifest actually delivered and authenticated. This is a boundary on the claim, not an impossibility result for every possible Candidate D construction.

The privacy claim also needs an explicit leakage function. At minimum, \(L\) must state whether the following are exposed: database size and epoch/root changes; publication and append timing; number of query rounds; padded query capacity; actual result cardinality; APIR-tree node-access count, timing and communication volume; AAD key or VRF-derived token; carrier and proof sizes; retries, aborts and acceptance outcomes; network addresses, accounts and device identifiers; and cross-service timing correlations. APIR's selective-failure protection applies within its own anytrust model; the composition must show that a detector, log and retrieval-server coalition cannot use rejection, padding changes or cross-epoch updates to learn the hidden predicate. Cryptographic query privacy must not be relabelled as network or account anonymity.

The current prior art leaves specific gaps:

1. **OMR/StOMR/UnifOMR:** private pertinence/retrieval is established under each paper's own model, but that is not a proof that a malicious detector returned every matching candidate. StOMR's passive/protocol-following model and UnifOMR's candidate-capacity ambiguity remain relevant.
2. **APIR (SRC-054):** multi-server point queries authenticate one record; predicate queries authenticate a weighted aggregate such as COUNT/SUM/AVG, not a variable-length enumeration. The anytrust model requires at least one honest replica. Its single-server construction is for point queries and requires an out-of-band database digest. The companion code is explicitly not production-ready; the inspected PGP implementation returns a scalar aggregate and leaves SUM unimplemented.
3. **AAD (SRC-055):** complete keyed lookup and append-only/fork-consistent evolution are established, but the lookup is not PIR-style private; zero-knowledge AAD is an explicit open problem. The formal construction uses q-SBDH/q-PKE assumptions and a trusted setup; the implementation's parameter estimate separately accounts for q-SDH and reports about 100-bit security (118 bits with BLS12-381), alongside high append/memory costs. The inspected C++ code exposes complete-membership and append-only proof paths but was not built or tested.
4. **Range APIR (SRC-056):** the indexed text claims soundness and completeness for variable-size private range results against a static owner-signed root. It also acknowledges leakage through query-dependent access count, timing and communication volume; the full PDF remains unavailable here, so its complete proof and cost cannot yet be assessed.
5. **Encrypted range-query schemes (SRC-059/060):** these are direct counterexamples to any broad claim that private, verifiable range search is absent. Their specific data structures, trust assumptions and proof scope differ; SRC-059 remains abstract/introduction-level, while SRC-060's formal privacy theorem is narrower than a separate malicious-cloud soundness proof.
6. **vPIR and multi-query vPIR (SRC-057/058):** local/global database-property proofs and same-database consistency across multiple point answers do not themselves enumerate every record matching a hidden predicate.
7. **SRC-052 integration:** an explicit service complete flag, slot-to-height map and retryable cursor address service-scoped range completeness and recovery; they are not cryptographic proofs of malicious-server completeness or sender-manifest closure.

Three construction paths remain hypotheses, each with a different failure boundary: (a) a **manifest-first** design gives the recipient an independent signed expected set, but still needs admission receipts and private retrieval; (b) a **log-first** AAD design gives snapshot-relative completeness but leaks the lookup key unless a private proof-retrieval layer is added; (c) **PIR-wrapped AAD proof retrieval** could hide which proof components are fetched, but must address variable proof length, dynamic root updates, query-count leakage and the cost of retrieving large proof material. Range APIR with fixed-capacity padding is another comparator, not an established solution: its leakage and dynamic-update behaviour require full-paper review and a concrete bound.

**Cost is not an afterthought.** APIR reports low user-time/bandwidth overhead for its predicate-query construction but at least 350× the computational work of its point-query path; its single-server point construction is 30–100× more computationally expensive than the multi-server path. AAD reports a 95 KiB average lookup proof (94 KiB in the abstract) for a key with 32 values at a dictionary size of \(10^6\), about 2.5 seconds to verify, append times measured in seconds, and 263 GiB RAM for the lookup-proof benchmark at size \(2^{20}-1\); the implementation's security estimate is about 100 bits under its evaluated parameters (118 bits with BLS12-381). The range-APIR full PDF remains inaccessible, so its exact proof/communication cost cannot be compared responsibly. Any proposed composition must count proof bytes, per-record/per-node queries, rounds, server work, snapshot-update work, padding/dummy accesses, and abort/retry behaviour rather than adding headline efficiencies from incompatible experiments.

**Disposition:** Candidate D remains a research hypothesis. A useful result must provide a construction with a formal composition proof, a rigorous impossibility/trade-off result, or a negative feasibility result showing why the required privacy/completeness/admission properties cannot coexist under stated assumptions. Merely naming APIR, AAD, OMR and a manifest is not a contribution.
