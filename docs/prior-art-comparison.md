# Prior-Art Comparison: Recipient Retrieval and Covert-Carriage Pipelines

**Checkpoint:** 2026-11-15  
**Role:** Working literature synthesis for W0.3/W6  
**Status:** Theory only · No implementation · Not audited.

## 1. Purpose and inspection boundary

This comparison tests the current candidate contributions against the closest identified work. It is intended to prevent novelty-by-combination and to expose where apparently similar systems solve different problems under different observer assumptions.

The following full texts have been inspected for their stated models, constructions, threat assumptions, evaluation and limitations:

- Liu and Tromer (2022), *Oblivious Message Retrieval* (OMR), including its formal definitions, DoS model, key unlinkability, performance and integration sections.
- Liu, Tromer and Wang (2024), *PerfOMR*, including its adapted OMR definition, adversary model, constructions, benchmarks and Zcash integration discussion.
- Shuhan, Baldimtsi and Ateniese (2026), *Oblivious Signaling*, including the formal receiver-privacy model, server leakage, inbox update construction, cost model and limitations.
- Lee and Yeo (2026), *SophOMR*, including its receiver-linkage model, SIMD-aware compression construction and evaluation.
- Liang et al. (2026), *InstantOMR*, including its OMR threat model, hybrid construction, benchmark methodology and latency/throughput trade-offs. The conference paper defers some formal details and malicious-detector correctness/integrity discussion to its full version; those details are not treated as verified here.
- Bauer et al. (2024), the full version of *Leveraging Generative Models for Covert Messaging: Challenges and Tradeoffs for “Dead-Drop” Deployments*, including its MBFTE construction, rendezvous/key-table arrangement, platform constraints, discovery hints, threat model and empirical evaluation.
- Bauer, Bao and Bindschaedler (2025), *Provably Secure Covert Messaging Using Image-based Diffusion Processes*, including its problem model, pre-shared-key assumption, adversary model, latent-space indistinguishability argument, proof discussion and empirical evaluation.

This is a focused comparison, not a systematic literature review, independent reproduction, proof verification or complete account of all related systems. Full-text inspection means the paper was examined; it does not mean its implementation or claims were independently reproduced.

## 3. Findings that change the novelty assessment

### 3.1 Recipient privacy is already a mature design space

OMR is not simply a generic “hidden inbox” idea. It has formalised message-recipient privacy, malicious-party collusion models, key unlinkability and DoS-resistance variants. PerfOMR, SophOMR and InstantOMR continue to optimise different parts of its cost profile. Oblivious Signaling changes where the expensive privacy work occurs, shifting cost from recipient retrieval to sender-triggered updates.

Consequently, the thesis cannot claim to introduce private recipient retrieval, recipient-message unlinkability, or the general idea of outsourcing discovery to an untrusted service. It must compare the actual alternatives and their different assumptions and cost models.

### 3.2 “Whole-pipeline” analysis is not sufficient by itself

The 2024 dead-drop paper explicitly moves beyond the steganographic core and examines a deployable public-platform pipeline: format transformation, record framing, fragmentation, platform constraints, rendezvous, overt/covert hints, retrieval, user detection and empirical trade-offs.

It therefore overlaps directly with a broad claim that this project is novel merely because it considers the entire delivery lifecycle. Its exclusions are important: the paper assumes the adversary cannot control the platform, observe fine-grained user actions or obtain IP identifiers, and leaves platform cooperation outside scope. Its text-generation channel and record construction also differ from the proposed image-carried protected-object route.

The distinction, if any, must be stated more precisely than “we consider the full pipeline.”

### 3.3 Image-based covert communication exists, but the delivery problem differs

The 2025 diffusion paper provides a formal image-based covert communication construction. It embeds information in generated images' initial latents, assumes a pre-shared key, and analyses a strong distinguisher that knows the model and can invert images. Its formal claim is tied to latent-space distribution preservation and its stated adversary.

However, the authors explicitly leave image delivery to the recipient outside their problem. Their channel is a generated image, not an arbitrary existing image carrying an independently protected capsule. It also prioritises small, high-security messages rather than frequent, low-latency private messaging.

The project must therefore distinguish at least three different ideas: embedding into an existing image, generating an image whose latent state carries information, and carrying an already protected object through an image-based route. They are not interchangeable.

### 3.5 Multi-image fragmentation and batch steganalysis are established comparators

Wang et al. (2024; SRC-043) directly demonstrate a multi-image fragmented covert-communication framework: a text file is divided into fragments; fragment numbers and content are embedded in images; each fragment is redundantly carried in multiple images; and the receiver reconstructs using sufficient carriers and majority voting over redundant copies. The authors explicitly distinguish steganography from watermarking and use robust DFT/DCT watermarking as the carrier mechanism. This is strong prior art against generic claims to multi-image fragmentation, redundant image carriage or subset-based reconstruction. It is not evidence of endpoint-authenticated message-set semantics, conversation continuity, sender identity or stealth against collection-level analysis.

Ker's batch-steganography work (SRC-044) studies pooling detector evidence across a fixed batch. The separate capacity theorem (SRC-045) establishes an asymptotic square-root bound under explicit assumptions: equal cover capacities, a shift-hypothesis detector response with regularity conditions, independent per-cover errors and batch size known to both parties. The theorem's bounds may be loose; neither work supplies a universal quantitative law for current social-platform images, dependent collections, robust watermarking or sequential acquisition.

**Research consequence:** The project must not claim novelty in multi-image fragmentation or redundant fragment recovery. It must evaluate the whole collection as well as individual carriers, distinguish robust watermarking from steganographic concealment, and ask whether authenticated message-set/context handling, observer-specific linkage analysis or route-neutral package semantics produce a narrower and measurable difference. That remains an open question, not an originality claim.

### 3.4 Privacy claims must be compared at the observer boundary

The reviewed systems protect different relationships:

- OMR-family systems protect message-to-recipient linkage within a public-board/detector model.
- Oblivious Signaling protects receiver linkage at the server while relying on an external anonymous channel for sender anonymity and accepting timing/system leakage.
- The dead-drop pipeline studies whether covert posts and users can be detected on a public platform, but excludes some platform and network observations.
- Diffusion-based covert messaging studies whether an image-generation process reveals embedded communication, while leaving delivery outside scope.

A single phrase such as “transport privacy” or “unlinkability” would conceal these differences. The thesis needs an explicit observer/property matrix: who observes which event, what they can correlate, what they can alter, which parties may collude, and which leakage is deliberately outside the model.

### 3.6 Active sanitization is a distinct adversarial route condition

Zhu et al. (2021; SRC-036) describe a learned attack/optimisation network intended to remove hidden data while preserving image quality. Wei et al. (2022; SRC-046) describe a general secret-data remover for robust steganography and watermarking, including image fusion and latent renewal. Zhu et al. (2023; SRC-047) describe a scaling-aware sanitization framework with Scaling-Net and SC-Net plus a discriminator. These papers establish active payload-removal as a researched countermeasure class; they do not establish that any named platform deploys these models or that the methods defeat every hiding scheme.

Zhu et al. (2025; SRC-048) then describe a counter-adaptation: a secret-seed-conditioned decoder is joined with a sanitizing network, and the sender iteratively optimises the cover so the sanitiser's output yields the intended secret to a receiver with the seed. This is not evidence that an unknown or changing sanitizer can be defeated. It changes the experiment: sanitizer knowledge, version, access, training assumptions and receiver side information become explicit adversary parameters.

**Research consequence:** Separate passive recompression, active sanitization and sender adaptation to a known sanitizer. A successful carrier extraction under one declared model is not a general robustness guarantee; neither extraction nor concealment establishes endpoint authenticity or message privacy.

### 3.7 Multi-frame GIF redundancy is not multi-image message fragmentation

Wang et al. (2026; SRC-049) provide a distinct carrier topology: one animated GIF with 16 frames, with bit repetition within each frame and majority voting across frames. Inspection of the author-linked README and selected code shows per-frame key/nonce material, a local key/ground-truth sidecar workflow, a sender path that writes frame PNGs, and a receiver that asserts the full 16-frame/key set. Missing-frame erasure tolerance is not established. The inspected StreamCipher uses raw ChaCha20 with no authentication tag visible in that path; a 32-bit NumPy PRNG fallback is labelled TEST ONLY. These code-level observations are not a security audit; the paper's formal security claims and result tables have not been inspected.

This is one animated carrier object with internal frame redundancy. It is not a set of separate image posts, an endpoint-authenticated fragment manifest, or a conversation of distinct logical messages. It therefore sharpens the terminology rather than displacing the multi-image prior-art comparison.

**Research consequence:** Compare frame-level loss/distortion and cross-frame recovery separately from separate-object fragmentation, account/time-window collection analysis and conversation linkage. Do not equate frame count with carrier count or message count.

## 5. Research question under examination

The remaining research question is whether carrying a common endpoint-protected message through ordinary image platforms produces decision-relevant security, privacy, reliability or usability properties that differ from file/attachment, conventional messaging and mixnet-backed routes.

This question is open. It does not assume that the image route is superior, original or even preferable. The literature already includes robust social-platform image steganography, platform-specific transformation studies, active payload-removal approaches and broad steganalysis surveys (SRC-033–SRC-037 and SRC-043–SRC-049). The comparison must therefore separate carrier extraction, detectability, endpoint authenticity/integrity, discovery privacy, observer leakage and operational cost. A result may favour another route or show that the image route has no defensible advantage.

## 6. What remains before any originality statement

1. Compare the exact observer capabilities and leakage functions of OMR, PerfOMR, SophOMR, InstantOMR and Oblivious Signaling in one table.
2. Compare the dead-drop paper's rendezvous, hints, platform assumptions, user-detection analysis and fragmentation against the proposed image-carriage lifecycle.
3. Compare the diffusion paper's latent-space security model with the threat model for an existing-image carrier and platform transformation.
4. Search for additional integrated anonymous messaging/dead-drop/image-carriage systems and related work that may challenge the proposed gap.
5. Define the project's intended deployment and primary observer model narrowly enough to make comparison meaningful.
6. Identify at least one non-obvious cross-layer result or analytical method that would remain useful even if image carriage is not the best route.
7. Revise the central research question and contribution language only after those steps.

## 8. Supplementary prior-art comparison · GOMR and Remise

The full-text comparison above has been extended after locating two important systems that materially narrow the candidate contribution.

### 8.1 Group Oblivious Message Retrieval (Liu, Tromer and Wang, 2024)

GOMR generalises OMR from one recipient to ad-hoc and fixed groups. It formalises group recipient privacy, detection-key unlinkability and denial-of-service resistance, and evaluates FHE/lattice-based constructions. Its model distinguishes public bulletin-board publication from the oblivious detector's retrieval task. It assumes the application payload is already suitably encrypted; authenticated clue-key distribution, contact discovery and privacy-preserving board posting remain outside scope. Its privacy adversary may control or collude with parties other than the protected sender and recipient(s), while the paper explicitly treats integrity and DoS under their own adversary-strength conditions.

**Research consequence:** Any broad claim that recipient-private delivery or group retrieval is newly addressed is untenable. Group communication must be included in the related-work baseline even if the proposed project remains focused on one-to-one communication.

### 8.2 Remise (Ravi, Shukla and Vadapalli, 2026)

Remise is a two-server DORAM-based anonymous communication system with two modes: an epoch-published anonymous bulletin board and private communication channels with writer-controlled read authorisation and revocation. It explicitly considers a global network adversary observing client-server communications, uses cover traffic, and hides access indices and writer-reader relationships under its two-server model. Its privacy requires the servers not to collude; integrity assumes both servers follow the protocol, and the paper assumes semi-honest servers. RemiseCC uses bearer authorisation tokens distributed out of band; privacy-preserving token distribution remains open. The authors also note that a malicious server can drop writes.

**Research consequence:** Remise directly overlaps any generic claim to have newly combined anonymous publication, authorised recipient access, relationship privacy and revocation. It does not provide an image-carriage or social-platform media pipeline, carrier metadata/forensic analysis, the proposed endpoint message semantics or human behavioural correlation model. The distinction must be argued at these exact boundaries, not by saying the project considers more of the lifecycle.

### 8.3 Revised cross-system judgement

The [observer/property matrix](observer-property-matrix.md) now compares OMR/GOMR, the OMR performance variants, Oblivious Signaling, Remise, dead-drop covert messaging and image-diffusion covert messaging across publication, discovery, retrieval, collection, carrier transformation, decapsulation, endpoint validation and human interaction.

The additional papers make three distinctions essential:

1. **Access-pattern privacy is not media-carrier privacy.** DORAM and OMR-family work hide selected access or recipient relationships under their models; they do not establish that an external image platform cannot correlate media, users or retrieval events.
2. **Anonymous access control is not endpoint-authenticated message semantics.** A system may hide which reader accesses a stored item without defining the project's peer identity verification, sender authentication, authenticated message context or capsule validation.
3. **A combined lifecycle narrative is not itself a contribution.** GOMR and Remise already cover substantial publication, retrieval, relationship and access-control machinery. The only defensible path for Candidate B is to identify a concrete cross-layer leakage or incompatible assumption arising when those privacy mechanisms meet media-carrier transformation, endpoint validation and human/network observation.

The research question in §5 remains open and must be answered from route-specific evidence. The comparison must be allowed to favour a simpler route or find no distinct image-carriage benefit.

## 9. Integrated messaging comparator · Echomix / Katzenpost

The full Echomix paper (SRC-032) materially changes the comparison. It is an integrated messaging architecture, not a narrow transport primitive. It combines a global and active adversary model; gateways, layered mixes and services; traffic/decoy coupling; anonymous service access; BACAP capability-derived pseudorandom storage locations; Pigeonhole asynchronous messaging; couriers and replicas; reliability and all-or-nothing backfills; tombstones; role-collusion analysis; and post-quantum packet variants.

### 9.1 Direct overlap

Echomix already covers several areas that the project previously treated as potentially differentiating when joined together: network-level anonymity, messaging semantics, recipient discovery through capability-derived addresses, asynchronous storage, access permissions, retry/reliability signalling and human online-routine correlation. It also analyses the cost and residual leakage of these mechanisms. The project must not claim a broad lifecycle privacy integration or a general responsibility-separated delivery architecture as new.

### 9.2 Important technical lessons

- **Traffic coupling is stronger than merely adding cover traffic.** Echomix identifies a final-hop asymmetry in Loopix where application traffic reaches a designated provider while decoys are distributed differently. The lesson is to inspect the complete path, including service edges, rather than infer protection from the presence of decoys.
- **Discovery is a cryptographic protocol responsibility.** BACAP derives pseudorandom box IDs and per-message capabilities; Pigeonhole uses those for storage and asynchronous retrieval. A generic changing locator is not an equivalent privacy construction.
- **Reliability signalling leaks information.** Acknowledgements, retries, polling and read/write timing can reveal online state or relationships. Echomix uses courier separation, controlled retries and opportunistic user-triggered acknowledgement/backfill, while acknowledging residual leakage.
- **Human behaviour remains relevant.** The paper explicitly discusses statistical disclosure from correlated online habits. The project must not present human correlation as an unexplored category.
- **Guarantees have costs and boundaries.** Continuous traffic, latency, bandwidth, finite retention, query patterns and particular colluding role combinations remain part of the security and usability account.

### 9.3 What may remain distinct

The remaining comparison boundary includes ordinary image-platform carriage of a common endpoint-protected message object. Echomix does not centre user-selected image carriers passing through social-platform transformations and account/hosting observations. However, dedicated prior art already studies robust image payloads on social platforms and active removal (SRC-033–SRC-036). The potentially distinct question is therefore not whether image payloads can survive or be hidden, but whether a complete endpoint-protected delivery route has measurable properties that differ from simpler and stronger baselines. This is a difference to evaluate, not evidence of originality.

The next work should compare the same endpoint message semantics across image carriage, file/attachment carriage, conventional messaging and a mixnet-backed messaging baseline. Each route must retain its own native guarantees, observer assumptions and costs. The evaluation should establish whether image carriage introduces a distinct, measurable benefit or burden, rather than simply describe a route not present in Echomix.

See the [Echomix comparator](echomix-katzenpost-comparator.md), [observer/property matrix](observer-property-matrix.md) and [route-comparison framework](route-comparison-framework.md) for the detailed boundary comparison.

### 10.1 What this crosswalk does and does not establish

The table shows that the mapped building blocks are not new: private retrieval and group pertinence, stateful OMR, OMR/PIR efficiency constructions, anonymous access control, covert public-platform pipelines, image-based covert channels, multi-image fragmentation, capability-addressed storage, copy batching and application-level incomplete-set gating all have prior art. The endpoint acceptance sequence is also a familiar protocol-design discipline when stated generically. StOMR's full paper and selected public artifact source are now inspected; UnifOMR remains abstract-level evidence only, pending full-text access.

The remaining possible value is narrower: a **reproducible cross-route evaluation method** that holds endpoint message semantics constant while refusing to erase differences in native units, partial recovery, storage atomicity, application display, service-reported events and observer assumptions. It may expose an incompatibility or evidence gap that changes route choice; it may also show that no special image-route benefit exists. Neither result is established yet.

A downstream UnifOMR integration provides a useful implementation-level pressure test (SRC-052). Its server returns an explicit digest-completeness flag and slot-to-height map, stops before cache holes, truncates at whole-height boundaries, and lets the client clamp its scan cursor so uncovered heights are retried. It then uses striped batch PIR for matching payloads and retains full-window trial-decryption fallback. This is an existing application-level completeness/recovery pattern, although it is not an arbitrary multi-carrier manifest or endpoint-acceptance transaction. The integration is third-party and experimental: its default server build excludes the OMR feature, its own product notes say OMR is opt-in/not maintained until mainnet contracts are live, and funded end-to-end checks remain open. These are repository self-reports, not independently reproduced deployment evidence.

The method must answer a concrete question, not simply populate a matrix: for a stated use case and observer, can the endpoint distinguish partial carrier recovery, a complete authenticated message, a service acknowledgement, a successful storage write and human receipt? Which route can support the required distinction, at what cost, and with what evidence? If the answer is already fully provided by the closest prior art or does not change a decision, Candidate C should be narrowed or retired. In particular, generic range-completeness flags, slot maps, whole-unit truncation and retryable cursors are not new; any surviving contribution must show what is missing for cross-carrier logical-message acceptance and why that distinction changes route choice.


### 10.2 Completeness is typed and scoped

A boolean named `complete` is not meaningful without its object, scope, authority, observation point and failure semantics. The following scopes are related but not interchangeable:

| Completeness / acceptance scope | What is being claimed? | Suitable evidence / authority | Comparator evidence | What it does **not** establish |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Carrier inventory** | The expected carrier objects for a publication or collection are present and retrievable. | Authenticated inventory/manifest plus independently observed or retrieved carrier objects. | Image-platform studies and SRC-043 study carriers/extraction; Dropbox identifies one shared file object. | That fragments are authentic, that a logical set is complete, or that every expected item was published. |
| **Fragment membership** | Each extracted piece belongs to the same intended message/set, recipient and protocol context. | Endpoint-authenticated manifest or equivalent authenticated membership relation. | SRC-043 uses numbered fragments but does not establish the thesis's authenticated manifest/identity binding; Nighthawk's slot-to-height map maps detected messages to chain heights, not fragments to a logical-message manifest. | That all required members exist or have been retrieved. |
| **Fragment-set completeness** | All required unique members, or the explicitly declared recovery threshold, are present with no unresolved conflict. | Authenticated manifest cardinality/order plus endpoint-verified membership and coding policy. | SRC-043 permits partial byte recovery; Pigeonhole specifies AllOrNothing copy semantics; neither is equivalent to the thesis's arbitrary cross-carrier authenticated manifest acceptance. | That reconstructed bytes are valid, fresh or attributable to a unique human sender. |
| **Byte reconstruction** | Some or all bytes can be recovered from the available pieces. | Decoder output, recovery count and explicit missing-data representation. | SRC-043 majority-votes redundant copies and marks missing bytes with `?`; reported success is bytes restored / total bytes. | Authentication, set membership, freshness or logical-message acceptance. |
| **Storage-operation atomicity** | An associated write/copy operation becomes visible all-at-once or not at all. | Storage transaction/commit semantics plus reader-visible observation. | Pigeonhole's specification claims reader-side AllOrNothing semantics; the inspected server path writes sequentially without rollback in the early-failure path. Actual concurrent reader visibility remains untested. | That a multi-carrier message is complete or accepted by the endpoint. |
| **Application continuity gate** | The application exposes a sequence only when its required contiguous chain is present. | Application's authenticated ordering/chain rule and display-state evidence. | katzenqt 0.0.2 gates conversation-log dispatch on a terminal F plus a complete contiguous decodable chain; pieces are still stored separately. | Atomic storage, complete publication or human receipt. |
| **Retrieval-window coverage** | The service has evaluated the requested range without a cache hole or resource truncation. | Service-reported range boundary, completeness flag and slot/record map, checked against the service's declared cache scope. | SRC-052's UnifOMR integration returns `complete` and `slot_heights`, stops before cache holes, truncates at whole-height boundaries and lets the client retry the uncovered tail. | That every expected message was published, that the index is globally complete, or that a logical message's full fragment set was retrieved. |
| **Endpoint logical-message acceptance** | The endpoint has authenticated the intended set, established required completeness, reconstructed and validated the protected object, checked freshness/replay policy and committed acceptance. | Explicit endpoint acceptance transition grounded in authenticated manifest/member evidence and validation result. | The thesis state machine defines this as a design obligation; none of the comparators above establishes the entire cross-carrier acceptance contract as stated here. | That a human saw, understood or acknowledged the message. |
| **Publication/index completeness** | Every expected object was published and the recipient's candidate view did not omit an expected item. | Independently checkable publication commitment/transparency or a complete authenticated index under a stated authority. | The inspected image, Dropbox, Signal, OMR and Pigeonhole evidence does not establish a universal end-to-end publication-completeness proof. | Endpoint acceptance or human receipt. |
| **Human receipt** | A person actually saw or acknowledged the content. | Explicit user action/receipt with its own authentication and privacy analysis. | No cited protocol/service event establishes comprehension or human authorship merely from storage, retrieval or display. | Identity or understanding beyond the precisely recorded user action. |

The practical consequence is that **retrieval-window completeness is not fragment-set completeness**, and neither is storage atomicity, application continuity or endpoint acceptance. SRC-052's `complete=true` is scoped to the service's cached range and truncation status; it is not a signed claim that every expected message was published. Pigeonhole's AllOrNothing label concerns a storage/copy operation under its specification; it does not itself authenticate a multi-image set. katzenqt's display gate concerns application continuity; it does not make storage atomic. SRC-043's byte recovery can be partial without a valid logical message. These distinctions must survive into the thesis terminology and evaluation records.

A surviving contribution cannot merely rename these existing mechanisms as a generic acceptance pipeline. It must identify a property not already supplied by the closest prior art—most plausibly authenticated cross-carrier manifest membership and endpoint acceptance of the complete reconstructed logical object—and show why that property changes a concrete route decision or closes a material failure/evidence gap. If no such result survives, Candidate C should be narrowed to an evidence-comparison method or retired.
